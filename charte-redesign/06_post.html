What is CBT, and how can it help you?

HD
Howard Dobrushin, LPC, CCBT, NCC
📅 March 2025 ⏱ 6 min read

Cognitive behavioral therapy — CBT — is one of the most extensively researched forms of psychotherapy in the world. Developed in the 1960s by psychiatrist Aaron Beck, it is built on a simple but powerful insight: the way we think about situations directly shapes how we feel and how we behave.

"Psychotherapy doesn't have to feel clinical or distant. At its best, it's a conversation that helps you see yourself more clearly."

At CHARTE Center, CBT has been a cornerstone of our therapeutic approach since we opened our doors in 2001. We've seen it help children, teenagers, adults, and older individuals navigate everything from everyday anxiety to complex co-occurring diagnoses involving intellectual and developmental disabilities.

How CBT works

CBT is structured and goal-oriented. Rather than spending months exploring the distant past, it focuses on what's happening now — the thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that are creating difficulty in your life today. A therapist and client work together to identify patterns of thinking that may be unhelpful or distorted, and then practice new ways of responding.

The cognitive triangle

At the heart of CBT is the relationship between three things: thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. Each influences the others. A negative thought ("I always fail") can lead to a feeling (shame) which drives a behavior (avoiding the task) — which then reinforces the original thought. CBT helps you interrupt that cycle.

Who can benefit from CBT?

CBT has strong evidence behind it for a wide range of conditions. It is one of the first-line treatments recommended for anxiety disorders, depression, OCD, PTSD, and phobias. At CHARTE Center, we also use adapted forms of CBT with clients on the autism spectrum and with intellectual disabilities, tailoring the pace, structure, and materials to each individual.

Common conditions CBT is used for
✓
Anxiety disorders, including generalized anxiety and social anxiety
✓

✓

What to expect in a CBT session

Sessions are typically 45–50 minutes and follow a loose structure. You and your therapist will usually begin by checking in on the week, review any between-session practice, work on a skill or explore a specific challenge, and close by agreeing on something to try before the next appointment. It's collaborative — you're an active participant, not a passive recipient.

Many people notice meaningful changes within 8–16 sessions, though this varies widely depending on the individual and what they're working through. Some clients continue for longer; others use a short course to build skills they then apply independently.

Starting CBT at CHARTE Center

If you're curious whether CBT might be right for you or someone in your family, the first step is simply getting in touch. We'll talk through what you're looking for, explain how we work, and match you with the right clinician. There's no pressure and no commitment required to have that first conversation.
